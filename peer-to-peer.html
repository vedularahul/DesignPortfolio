Peer-to-peer engagement

Peer engagement is how a learner interacts with the people around them across their journey. Ours mostly ended when the live class did — so we went looking for every point where it could start again.

What 1,500 learners said

Qualitative research and focus groups across a sample of more than 1,500 learners, drawn deliberately from people who were struggling: completion under 20%, or who had skipped ahead to the last course. Mixed demographics, all in India.

85% said they would prefer to study in a group. 60% wanted to know where they stood relative to their peers, because that tells them how much further they have to go.

The rest of the findings describe a product that goes quiet the moment a session ends. No engagement outside live sessions. Delivery that is monotonous and one-way. No assistance at the moment someone is stuck on a project. No notification when something relevant happens in the community.

A Six Sigma learner described the contact with the class ending with the class, and interest draining away afterwards. A cyber security learner connected it directly to procrastinating on project submission.

What the literature already knew

Secondary research across academic publications and competitor products pointed the same way. Learners who take part in forums are over 25% more likely to complete a course. Spider-web discussions before a live class raise engagement in it. Self-introduction during an induction session produces engagement that happens naturally afterwards. Giving learners roles motivates a cohort. Polls, and discussion of the polls, work.

Sources were Northern Illinois University's teaching and learning guidance, and Coursera's work on the drivers of quality in online learning.

Mapping the journey to find the gaps

We mapped activities, goals, pain points and business opportunities across the whole journey — pre-learning, learning, post-learning — and marked what was missing at each touchpoint.

  • Onboarding: learners in a cohort don't know their peers, their trainers, or anyone's background.
  • Core learning: self-learning content is consumed in silos; live classes run long, monotonous and one-way, with no group activity inside them.
  • Projects: people need support at the moment they're working, and there is none.
  • Post learning: no route to alumni who have already got the job, and the tips that come with them.

Ideas went through a decision matrix scoring feasibility against impact, so the rollout order was argued rather than asserted. The metrics we set were platform stickiness as the north star — weekly over monthly actives — plus quality of interactions and posts, and total learner interaction time.

What we built

Know your peers. Introducing yourself became part of onboarding and part of the welcome class, rather than an optional forum people never find.

Discussion where the work is. Q&A threads sit directly beneath self-learning videos, with history retained and filters by cohort, top posts and recency, so the section gets richer over time instead of resetting. Live discussion rooms run per project, assessment and video, showing how many peers are in each one.

Discussion during projects. The most requested feature from the learner support group — live rooms and threads attached to the project itself, at the exact moment people get stuck.

Recognition worth having. Certificates auto-post into the community thread so a cohort can congratulate someone, with an option to share outward. Leaderboards, points and polls give the 60% who wanted to know where they stand a way to see it.

The features people asked for weren't social features. They were a way of not being alone at 11pm with a project due.

What shipped, and what it moved

Live on the platform: learner introductions during the cohort, open discussion rooms, and discussion inside the self-learning videos. The discussion rooms were the clear favourite.

Session CSAT moved from 3.9 to 4.2. Deeper community integration was still in design when the work handed over.
