Who is actually arriving

We pulled purchase data and mobile heatmaps over three months and found four distinct people using the same menu.

  • Students, 18–21, there to learn. A quarter of them name learning itself as the objective. They buy technology, project management, Agile and Scrum, big data, cyber security.
  • Early professionals, 21–25, and the most single-minded group on the site — 37% are there to find a better job. Technology, PM, data science and business analytics, cloud.
  • Mid-level professionals, 25–35, split between finding a better job and growing in the one they have. PM, Agile and Scrum, data science, business and leadership.
  • Senior professionals and leaders, 35–40, almost entirely focused on growth in the current role. Business and leadership, IT service and architecture, cloud.

A single flat list of the catalogue serves none of them. A student and a senior leader arriving at the same menu need different first rows, and the old navigation gave them the same one.

Two kinds of change

Secondary research, competitive analysis and the existing data gave us hypotheses in two buckets, which is a useful split because they get tested and shipped differently.

Modular level

  • Group courses by the categories each persona actually buys, rather than by internal taxonomy.
  • Promote best-selling and popular courses.
  • Add a contextual “Explore category” action.
  • Introduce reasons to believe, social proof and category benefits, to carry credibility inside the menu itself.

Interface level

  • Make elements inside the mega menu functional on click.
  • Introduce scroll, so the menu scales as the catalogue grows instead of breaking.
  • Turn every course touchpoint into a card rather than a text link.
  • Tighten proximity and consistency throughout.

Scalability was a design constraint here, not an engineering one. A catalogue that keeps growing will eventually break any menu that assumes it can show everything.

What moved

Click-through rose 15%, and the traffic-to-lead ratio on targeted program pages went from 2.3% to 3.7%. Applied across roughly four million monthly visitors, that's the change that mattered.
