The short version

R Discovery helps researchers find and keep up with papers in their field. The home screen was built around that second job — a personalised feed of recommended reading, journals to follow, topics to browse.

Feeds are a reasonable model for open-ended discovery. But a researcher opening the app usually isn't browsing. They have a citation to chase, a claim to verify, or a gap to check before submitting. They arrive with a query already formed, and the interface asks them to scroll past someone else's suggestions to find the box where they can type it.

The redesign inverted the hierarchy. Search became the primary object rather than one module among several. The feed moved to where it belongs — after the query, as the thing you fall back to when you don't have a specific question.

Users focusing on the search bar rose from 2.6k to 8.7k, a 234.6% increase. The share navigating through to the search screen went from 25% to 54%. The numbers are large because the old design was suppressing a behaviour people already wanted, rather than creating a new one.

The cheapest interaction wins are the ones where you stop blocking something people were already trying to do.
