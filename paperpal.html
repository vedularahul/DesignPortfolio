Researchers don't write where you want them to

Paperpal helps researchers and students write academic English: grammar, clarity, citations, submission readiness. It had a good web editor. That was the problem.

A PhD student drafts in Word because their supervisor comments in Word. A lab collaborates in Google Docs. Anyone writing with equations is in Overleaf. Asking that person to move their manuscript into a different editor is asking them to change a habit built over years, at the exact moment they are most stressed about a deadline.

So the question stopped being how do we make our editor better, and became how does Paperpal show up inside the tools people already have open, without the product splintering into four codebases and four different experiences.

The same assistant, four places to meet it.

One architecture, four surfaces

The foundational piece was an information architecture for the web editor built to be portable rather than complete. Every feature had to survive being lifted into a sidebar a fraction of the width, in a host application whose own UI we didn't control.

That constraint did useful things to the design. Features that only made sense with a lot of screen real estate got reconsidered. Naming had to hold up without surrounding context. The hierarchy had to be readable in a narrow column.

The payoff is that a feature now ships once and appears everywhere consistently, and someone who learns Paperpal in Word already knows it in Overleaf.

New IA instances for Paperpal 2026

Onboarding by intent, not by tour

Paperpal does a lot of things. A new user arriving on a generic tour of all of them learns nothing and activates nothing. Someone arriving to fix one paper before a Friday submission wants exactly one path.

We replaced the tour with an intent question at the start, asking what you came here to do, then routing people straight to the feature that answers it. Feature activation went up around 42 per cent.

Accessibility as an audience question

The editor revamp included compliance to AA level. That sounds like a checkbox exercise and it wasn't. Paperpal's users are researchers and students, a population that includes a lot of people reading for eight hours a day, plenty using screen readers, and many working in their second or third language.

We also moved the marketing surface onto Strapi, so landing page experiments could run without an engineering ticket each time. Design velocity on the acquisition side stopped being gated on the product roadmap.

Churn is a design surface

A researcher between papers doesn't want to keep paying, and the only button we offered them was cancel. Adding a genuine pause, plus winback flows for people who had already left, cut churn by around 12 per cent.

The gamified offer work that ran alongside this is written up separately in Spin the Wheel.

Research in the places research happens

Most of this came out of running studies inside universities and research institutions rather than over a video call. Watching someone fight their own manuscript on their own machine, in their own lab, surfaces things a moderated remote session never will, including which parts of the workflow happen entirely outside our product.

That work also went outward: workshops and a talk at IEEE APSCON at IIT Hyderabad, positioning Cactus as an AI first company in research tooling rather than a service vendor that added AI.

Design in a research tool is mostly the work of not interrupting someone who is already concentrating.

Where it stands

Paperpal is now used by researchers and students worldwide, across four surfaces, and played a part in a revenue journey of roughly $5.5 million. The work was recognised at the UX Design Awards 2026.

The part that still isn't finished is the handoff between automated help and human help. Getting that boundary right, knowing when to stop suggesting and start connecting someone with an editor, is the next real design problem.
