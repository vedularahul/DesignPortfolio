A month of transcripts

Primary research was a calendar month of real chat data — 35,416 transcripts from September — plus the requirements the product and sales teams brought to the table.

We read 10% of them by hand, 3,716 conversations spread across the month, and marked each one high or low quality. High meant the person was willing to share details to get course information. Low meant they dropped out mid-conversation.

59.15% came back low quality. Around 8,100 new leads came out of the month, of which 13.47% became paying users. The overwhelming majority of what people asked about was course details — duration, fees, eligibility, start dates — with only a few hundred LMS queries and a few dozen B2B ones.

Most of what reached a human was a question a well-built interface could have answered in four seconds.

What the good conversations had in common

Reading the high-quality chats gave us the actual shape of learner demand, and the tone the learning consultants used to get there. The same five things came up: course duration, number of courses, fee, eligibility, start date.

Alongside those, the business had its own goals — better-qualified leads, a CSAT rating captured after the chat, media support so links, PDFs and images could be sent, and an experience that didn't feel like a form.

Four competitors, one framework

Of around 13 competitors in the ed-tech sector, four had a chatbot. We mapped all four flows end to end. The pattern in the good ones was preset tappable options with contextual text entry, card-based course details, and a clean path to a human. The pattern in the bad ones was a bot that existed only to harvest a phone number, in a tone nobody would mistake for a person.

That gave us a framework with a clear division of labour. The bot holds intent, entity and context — work out what you want, capture the details, present preset information that fits. The human holds empathy, helpfulness and encouragement, and arrives already knowing who they are talking to.

Arya

The final flow opens with five options rather than a blank field, so nobody has to guess what the bot can do. It takes a name, then an area of interest, then offers programs that fit — with duration, number of courses, fee, eligibility and next cohort on the card, which is exactly the list the research produced.

Email, phone and whether you're a student or working come after the value, not before it. At the end, the learner chooses: connect to a counsellor now, or request a callback at a stated time. Either way the consultant opens the conversation already holding the context.

The number, and how it was going to be reached

Only 2.8% of chat initiations ended in a paying user, while 56.79% produced no lead at all. That's 21,459 conversations a month, and roughly 2,800 hours of sales time spent on them.

We planned the rollout in three phases rather than claiming the whole number at once — a monitored release in parts of India with a survey of people who used it, then a national rollout with multimedia support, then the rest. The reductions we were targeting rose across those phases from 40% to 75% to nearly all of it.
